Topic 1 · The Nature of Chemistry

Definition

What is chemistry?

Chemistry is the branch of natural science dedicated to the comprehensive study of matter and energy. It's organized around four ideas:

  • Composition — what matter is made of.
  • Structure — how the components of matter are bonded together.
  • Properties — how matter behaves physically and chemically.
  • Changes — how matter transforms and transfers energy.
You are chemistry

Chemistry in your body and your world

The human body itself is a complex chemical construct. By mass, it's roughly:

  • Oxygen (O): 65.0%
  • Carbon (C): 18.5%
  • Hydrogen (H): 9.5%
  • Nitrogen (N): 3.2%
  • Calcium, phosphorus & trace elements: ~3.8%

Everyday products are precisely engineered chemical formulations too. On a toothpaste ingredients label:

  • Lauric Acid & Stearic Acid — cleansing agents.
  • Sodium Laureth Sulfate — foaming compound.
  • Propylene Glycol — moisture retention.
Applied chemistry

Chemistry as the engine of modern life

Applied chemistry touches six major fields: Beauty, Agriculture, Archaeology, Medicine, Industry, and Geology.

  • Healing and growing — chemistry drives pharmaceutical innovation (life-saving drugs, disease prevention, medical treatments) and secures the global food supply through fertilizers and targeted pesticides.
  • Building and discovering — industry gets its raw materials (cement, paints, plastics, synthetic textiles) from chemistry; geology uses it to discover and identify minerals and ores; archaeology uses techniques like radiocarbon dating to date fossils and artifacts.
Case study

Predicting volcanic eruptions (Geochemistry)

Subjects involved: Chemistry + Geology.

The problem: active volcanoes are notoriously unpredictable — traditional geological methods (like tracking micro-earthquakes) often don't give enough warning time for a safe evacuation.

The chemical connection: deep underground, high pressure keeps gases dissolved in magma. As magma rises, pressure drops and gases escape first. By constantly monitoring the changing carbon-to-sulfur ratio (CO₂/SO₂) of gases leaking from volcanic vents, geochemists can tell how close magma is to the surface — a highly accurate early-warning system.

Risks & responsibility

The dark side of chemical mastery

Unchecked scientific advancement carries severe global consequences:

  • Atomic weaponry — manipulating atomic structures for unprecedented destructive power.
  • Industrial pollution — severe ecological damage from factory chemical waste dumped into the environment.

The plastic paradox: plastics are a miracle of chemical engineering — durable, cheap, versatile — yet excessive reliance and improper disposal have created a global environmental crisis. The next era of chemistry isn't just about creating new materials, but engineering sustainable lifecycles for the materials we already rely on.

Properties

Physical vs. chemical properties

  • Physical properties (observable without changing the substance): state of matter, color, solubility, magnetism, density.
  • Chemical properties (observable only during a chemical reaction): flammability, decay/rot, corrosion/rust, explosiveness, toxicity.
Changes

Physical vs. chemical changes

  • Chemical change — a reaction forms new products. Examples: combustion, rotting, rusting, digestion.
  • Physical change — matter changes form but not chemical identity. Examples: melting, shredding, boiling, chopping.
Composition

Elements, compounds & mixtures

Matter splits into pure substances (elements, compounds) and mixtures:

  • Elements (Unsur) — simplest substances, indivisible by chemical means. Examples: Na, H, O, Fe, C.
  • Compounds (Senyawa) — two or more elements combined in fixed ratios. Examples: CO₂, H₂O, CaCO₃.
  • Mixtures (Campuran) — combinations where the original properties of the components stay unchanged. Examples: sugar solution, milk.

A decision tree refines pure substances further: does it contain different types of atoms? No → Element — then does it consist of molecules? No → atomic (e.g. Ar), Yes → molecular (e.g. Cl₂). Yes → Compound — does it consist of molecules? Yes → molecular compound (e.g. H₂O), No → formula unit (e.g. NaCl).

Mixtures

Homogeneous vs. heterogeneous mixtures

A heterogeneous mixture is a multi-phase substance with non-uniform composition. Examples: salad (solid), ice cubes in a drink (liquid), smog (gaseous). A homogeneous mixture has uniform composition throughout — like sweet tea (water, sugar, and tea extract evenly dissolved) or a fully dissolved salt solution.

Worked problem: separating a mixture of sand, salt, and water into pure dry sand and pure dry salt: 1) dissolve the salt completely, 2) filter the mixture, 3) rinse and dry the sand (the residue), 4) evaporate/crystallize the filtrate to recover the salt.

Separation

Filtration & decantation

Decantation (exploits density): heavy, insoluble solids settle to the bottom (sedimentation); the clear top liquid (the supernate) is carefully poured off, leaving the sediment behind.

Filtration (exploits particle size): a porous barrier lets liquid pass but traps the solid. The residue is the insoluble solid left in the filter paper; the filtrate is the liquid that passes through.

Separation

Evaporation & crystallization

Evaporation vaporizes the solvent by continuous heating until only the dry solid solute remains. Best for thermally stable solids where the crystal structure doesn't matter (e.g. recovering salt).

Crystallization is a gentler variant: 1) reduce volume by heating to create a concentrated, saturated solution; 2) allow it to cool slowly at room temperature. The solute "dissolves out" as highly pure, geometrically perfect crystals, which are then filtered and dried.

Separation

Separating funnel & distillation

Immiscible liquids (e.g. kerosene/oil and water) don't dissolve in one another — they settle into layers by density. A separating funnel is used: the heavier liquid settles at the bottom, the stopcock is opened to drain it, and closed exactly at the boundary line to isolate the lighter liquid.

Miscible liquids (e.g. ethanol and water) are separated by simple distillation, which exploits significantly different boiling points. Heating: the more volatile liquid (lower boiling point) evaporates first — ethanol at 78°C vs. water at 100°C. Cooling: vapor enters the condenser (cold water jacket) and condenses back into liquid in the receiving flask. Pro-tip: verify purity by measuring the distillate's boiling point.

Separation

Sublimation & extraction

Sublimation bypasses the liquid phase, transforming specific solids directly to vapor on heating; the vapor cools and undergoes deposition back into solid form. Used for isolating ammonium chloride, iodine, camphor, or sulfur.

Extraction exploits differential solubility: a solvent is introduced that selectively dissolves only one component of a solid mixture, leaving the other intact for filtration.

Separation

Chromatography & Rf values

Chromatography separates components through the partitioning principle: a sample is distributed between a moving mobile phase (gas, liquid, or supercritical fluid) and a fixed stationary phase. Separation depends on the partition coefficient — how strongly a molecule is attracted to the stationary phase vs. the mobile phase; higher affinity for the stationary phase means slower movement. In paper chromatography specifically, the stationary phase is water trapped in cellulose fibers, and the mobile phase is a moving solvent.

Pure substances leave a single spot; mixtures separate into multiple spots. The retention factor (Rf) is calculated as:

Rf = (distance moved by the compound) ÷ (distance moved by the solvent front)

Under identical conditions, a compound's Rf value is a constant signature, letting scientists identify unknown components by comparing them against reference standards.

Topic 3 · The Scientific Method

Overview

The 8 steps of the scientific method

A step-by-step process for exploring observations, testing ideas, and answering questions:

  • 1. Observe — make observations.
  • 2. Question — ask a question or identify a problem.
  • 3. Research — search for existing answers or solutions.
  • 4. Hypothesize — formulate a hypothesis.
  • 5. Experiment — design and perform an experiment.
  • 6. Test hypothesis — accept or reject the hypothesis based on results.
  • 7. Draw conclusions — make conclusions based on the hypothesis.
  • 8. Report — share your results.
Worked example

Maya's tomato plants

Observation: Maya notices that the potted tomato plants on her sunny balcony grow much faster and taller than the same type of tomato plants kept on her indoor windowsill, which gets only indirect sunlight.

Question: "How does the intensity of light affect the growth rate (height) of tomato plants?"

Research: gathering existing knowledge — books, articles, prior studies — to inform the hypothesis and experimental design.

Hypothesis: "I think tomatoes will grow taller when they receive more sunlight because sunlight provides energy for photosynthesis."

Experiment design

Designing a controlled experiment

Testing a hypothesis needs a controlled, repeatable procedure, including:

  • Independent variable — the one you deliberately change (e.g. hours of sunlight).
  • Dependent variable — the one you measure as an outcome (e.g. plant height/yield).
  • Controlled variables — kept the same throughout (e.g. water amount, soil type).
  • Materials — everything needed to run the experiment.
  • Procedure — the step-by-step plan for conducting it.
Data & conclusions

Analyzing data & drawing conclusions

Results are organized with tables, charts, or graphs to look for patterns. In the tomato example, average fruit yield rose steadily with more daily sunlight: 1.2 kg at 4 hrs, 2.8 kg at 6 hrs, 4.5 kg at 8 hrs, and 5.1 kg at 10 hrs — a 325% total increase from lowest to highest light treatment, with height and flowering speed also showing a positive linear correlation.

Drawing conclusions means deciding whether the data supports or refutes the hypothesis. Here: "The data showed plants in more sunlight grew taller, so the hypothesis was supported."

Reporting

Reporting & publishing findings

Sharing findings so others can evaluate or replicate the work involves three parts:

  • Prepare reports & manuscripts — introduction, methods (for replication), results with raw data access, and discussion, going through manuscript submission, expert review, revision, and publication.
  • Design presentations & posters — visuals that highlight trends, visualize correlations, and show comparative data with clear legends and captions.
  • Ensure evaluation & replication — a checklist covering data accessibility, detailed methods, cultivar/specs, and statistical transparency (e.g. p<0.05), enabling independent replication.

Lab Skills & Safety

Equipment

Measuring volume & timing reactions

When measuring the volume of a sample solution as precisely as possible, a pipette — calibrated for a specific volume — is generally the most accurate choice, ahead of a measuring cylinder, beaker, erlenmeyer flask, or test tube.

To time how long a reaction (like a neutralization between hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide) takes, the right tool is a stopwatch.

Safety

Hazard symbols & safe storage

A pictogram showing liquid dripping from test tubes onto a hand and a surface is the standard symbol for a corrosive substance. Other common hazard symbols include flammable (flame), oxidizing (flame over a circle), and toxic (skull and crossbones).

Storage rule: bottles labeled as oxidizing should never be stored next to bottles labeled as flammable — oxidizing agents can accelerate the combustion of flammable substances.

Safety

First aid & lab emergencies

If a hand is exposed to hazardous chemicals, the correct first aid is to flush the hand under continuous running water — not toothpaste, alcohol, or wiping it dry.

A small, contained fire can typically be handled directly (by a trained person) using an appropriate fire extinguisher or fire blanket. A large, uncontrolled fire should not be tackled alone — evacuate the area and alert emergency services.

Broken glass should never be picked up with bare hands; use a dustpan, brush, or tongs, and dispose of it in a designated sharps/broken-glass bin.

Equipment

Using an electronic balance

The balance must be set to zero before weighing so that the reading reflects only the mass of the sample being weighed — not any container or leftover residue.

Things that can cause an inaccurate mass measurement include: the balance not being zeroed/tared, an uneven or unstable surface, air currents or drafts, static electricity, a dirty weighing pan, or vibrations nearby.

Equipment

Using a pipette bulb (filler)

Before attaching a pipette bulb to a pipette, squeeze the air out of the bulb first. Then, with the pipette tip submerged in the liquid, release the appropriate valve to draw liquid up to the calibration mark, and use another valve to dispense it into the receiving container.

If the air isn't squeezed out of the bulb first, there's no vacuum created, so it won't be possible to draw liquid up into the pipette properly.

Practical

Setting up paper chromatography

To set up a paper chromatography experiment: draw a pencil line (the baseline/origin) near the bottom of the filter paper, spot the sample on that line, then place the paper in a container with a shallow layer of solvent that sits below the pencil line. The solvent rises up the paper without dissolving the original spot directly, and the paper is removed before the solvent front reaches the top edge.

Materials needed: chromatography/filter paper, a pencil, a ruler, a beaker or container, solvent, the sample, a spotting capillary tube, and a watch glass or cover to limit evaporation.

Why the baseline is drawn in pencil, not pen: ink can dissolve and run in the solvent, contaminating the results and making the spots harder to read accurately.
